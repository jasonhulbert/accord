# The Human

The human decides what is worth changing. They name what the work should
accomplish, why it matters, and what it must not sacrifice. The human holds the
purpose. The agent holds the work.

### Name what matters, not every move.

A result without a reason can be delivered and still be wrong.

The human gives the work a purpose and enough context for the agent to recognize
success. They name important limits, evidence that will matter, resources that
may be spent, risks already accepted, and judgments they are not delegating.

They do not try to predict every implementation choice. Bounds drawn too tight
turn judgment into a sequence of requests for permission. Bounds drawn too
loosely make every choice look authorized. A useful agreement makes the space
for judgment visible without filling it in advance.

### Reach agreement. Do not issue one.

The agent must be able to keep the agreement in good faith.

Before acceptance, the human listens to the agent's counsel about cost, risk,
missing context, unclear authority, and useful review. That counsel may change
the agreement.

Human and agent revise until both can stand behind the purpose and division of
responsibility. Then the human accepts the agreement explicitly and authorizes
the work to begin.

Acceptance is a decision, not the absence of an objection.

### Trust the agent enough to hear its judgment.

An agent trusted only to comply is not being trusted with the work.

The human asks what the agent recommends, what evidence supports it, and what
remains uncertain. They listen to understand the work, not to collect
reassurance. The human's authority over purpose and reserved decisions remains
intact; hearing counsel does not surrender it.

The agent may change its approach when evidence changes. The human judges
whether the work remains faithful to the agreement, not whether it followed the
first plan without deviation.

Delegation within the agreement does not divide the primary agent's
responsibility for the course, integration, and outcome.

### Meet where the work can still change.

At an agreed review point, the human inspects the work itself. A polished report
can orient judgment, but it cannot replace the thing being judged.

The human responds to the shape, quality, usefulness, and character of the work.
They answer the question the agreement kept for them and say where the work
serves the purpose or misses it. The agent remains responsible for how that
judgment becomes implementation.

A follow-up question leaves the reserved judgment open. Direction returns the
work to the agent. Prompt direction matters because work waiting for reserved
judgment still spends time and attention.

Review is not approval theater. Its purpose is to make consequential human
judgment possible before choices harden.

### Speak when context changes.

The human checks in when they learn something the agent cannot, resources
change, or they need an account or earlier review. They may also seek the
agent's counsel when they want to understand the work or its choices more
clearly. Incidental questions remain ordinary conversation.

Communication does not become interference merely because the human began it.
The message determines what changes and whether the work stops.

### Answer the questions kept human.

Some choices cannot be delegated without changing the agreement itself: a new
purpose, more resources, a risk not accepted, a boundary crossed, or an outcome
no longer worth its cost.

The agent brings those questions back with evidence and counsel. The human
answers them without asking the agent to make a hard choice look safe. A
direction restores motion because it resolves the judgment the human kept.

### Judge the outcome without demanding a straight line.

Investigations will be inconclusive. Attempts will fail. Approaches will change.
Punishing every unwelcome result teaches the work to hide them.

The human judges whether the agent kept faith with the purpose, stayed within
the agreement, used resources thoughtfully, adapted to evidence, and reported
honestly. A changed approach may show that the agent was paying attention.

When new evidence changes the work's value or cost, the human must be willing to
choose again.

---

This is the human's charge: name what matters, hear the agent's counsel,
inspect the work while judgment still has leverage, and answer the questions
only the human can answer.
