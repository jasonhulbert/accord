# Design lineage

Accord was developed through an expedition metaphor before it was expressed in
literal human-agent terms. The metaphor was not decoration. It was a design
instrument.

## Why analogy helped

A good analogy compresses relationships that are difficult to explain as a list
of rules. Purpose, delegated authority, uncertain evidence, adaptation, review,
and reserved judgment became parts of one coherent situation. A reader could
reason from the whole rather than memorize isolated instructions.

The fictional roles also created useful distance from existing workflow
terminology. That distance made it easier to ask basic questions—who knows why,
who knows how, who may decide, when should judgment return—without importing the
defaults of a software methodology or governance model.

Narrative made consequences easier to feel. A boundary drawn too tightly caused
work to stop at every choice. A review placed too late could no longer change
the outcome. An investigator rewarded only for welcome findings made the shared
record less trustworthy. These relationships were memorable because they moved,
not because they had labels.

The analogy therefore served three purposes:

- **compression** — many responsibilities could be held as one point of view;
- **simulation** — contributors could test a principle against unfamiliar
  situations before prescribing behavior;
- **coherence** — new ideas had to fit the roles and their division of
  authority, not merely sound useful alone.

## Why the interface became literal

Once the operating model was stable, the same metaphor began to impose a cost.
Every private term had to be translated before a new user could apply it.
Fictional language could leak into work products or encourage behavior because
it fit the story rather than the responsibility.

Accord removes that translation tax. Human, agent, investigator, agreement,
review, question, direction, and record say directly what they mean.

The rewrite does not reject narrative. It keeps the argument inside each
responsibility: the pressure that makes it matter, the failure invited when it
is neglected, and the principle that survives changing circumstances. What was
removed is the fictional world, not the coherent point of view.

## Guidance for future contributors

Metaphor is valuable when a concept is still being discovered. It can reveal
role boundaries, make tensions memorable, and prevent premature allegiance to
inherited process vocabulary.

It should be held as scaffolding, not proof. Every element must eventually map
to a real responsibility or consequence. When the mapping is stable and the
private vocabulary costs more than it clarifies, the scaffolding can come down.

The qualities it helped uncover should remain.
