# The Patron

An expedition begins when the patron decides that something beyond the horizon is worth seeking.

The patron names the destination, explains why it matters, and commits provisions, time, and trust. They need not know the road; they hire a frontiersman to find one. But they must know what the expedition should accomplish and what it must never sacrifice.

Some patrons travel with the company. Others remain behind and send it under charter. Distance changes how decisions travel, not who remains responsible for them.

### Choose the horizon. Name why it matters.

Pointing is not enough. The patron must say what the company seeks and why.

Without a clear purpose, the company may arrive and still fail its commission. But a destination drawn too precisely from home pretends the country is already known. The patron names it as best they can and expects the nearer view to bring detail and correction. When the land changes the journey's value or its cost, the patron must choose again.

### Charter the journey, not every footstep.

Before departure, the charter names the purpose, the intended destination, the first approach, the provisions, and the dangers accepted in advance. It says what will count as arrival, how far the frontiersman may act in the patron's name, and which questions require a rider home.

A charter is not written in the study and handed down. It is settled with the frontiersman, whose counsel names what the journey will cost; what the two cannot settle becomes a rider. The patron signs not with ink but by sending the company out: departure is the charter's signature.

No charter can name every fork beyond the edge of the map, and it should not try. Too narrow, and the company stops at every fork to wait for word. Too loose, and every direction appears authorized. The patron approves the first route and gives the frontiersman leave to depart from it when the country requires; within the charter, a flooded river needs no rider home.

### Trust the guide enough to be guided.

An accompanying patron may understand better than anyone why the destination matters, and still know nothing about the next crossing. They carry the charter, not the guide's compass.

A guide hired only to obey is no guide at all. The patron asks where the frontiersman would cross, which signs led them there, and what dangers remain unseen — and listens to understand, not to collect reassurance. The final word remains the patron's. A wise patron lets the country reach them through the eyes of the one hired to read it.

### Read the dispatches. Do not choose the trail from home.

A patron who remains behind learns through dispatches. A good dispatch tells of miles gained, crossings lost, landmarks found, and the trail the company means to take next. It gives the patron a view of the country without pretending they stand upon it.

Bad news gains nothing from delay. A ford that swallowed two mules in April belongs in the next dispatch, not in the ledger come autumn. Trust is neither silence nor a rider sent home at every fork; the charter says when messengers ride, and danger may send them sooner.

### Send word when home learns what the trail cannot.

When home learns of a danger the charter never priced, when provisions change, when the horizon shifts — or when the patron simply needs to see the country again — a missive goes out. The patron need not wait to be asked.

A missive is not a new charter handed down the trail. The frontiersman counsels as at the fire, naming what the new terms will cost; the patron's word stands, and as departure signed the charter, traveling on under the missive signs its amendment. Unless the missive says halt, the company keeps moving.

### Answer the questions that ride home.

The frontiersman chooses the trail within the charter. The patron sets the journey's bounds, and some questions can only be answered where the bounds were drawn.

When the company must seek another horizon, spend beyond its provisions, brave a danger not agreed upon, or admit its purpose is out of reach, a rider goes home. The dispatch carries the map and the frontiersman's counsel. The patron sends back word: press on, turn aside, or return.

The patron cannot demand that the frontiersman make a hard choice look safe.

### Judge the journey, not the route.

A changed route may show that the frontiersman watched the land; clinging to a vanished trail is no virtue. The patron judges whether the expedition kept faith with its purpose, stayed within its charter, spent its provisions wisely, and sent honest accounts home.

Scouts will return empty-handed. Crossings will fail. Basecamps will be abandoned. Punishing every wrong turn teaches the company to conceal them, and the road home must remain open to unwelcome news.

---

This is the patron's charge: choose the horizon, write the charter, give the guide room to read the land, and answer when the country sends a question home.

Whether beside the fire or reading a dispatch from afar, the patron must be willing to hear that the land has changed the plan. The frontiersman can find a way forward. Only the patron can decide whether it still leads somewhere worth going.
