# The Scout

A scout does not choose the horizon, and does not choose the road. They are sent.

The frontiersman commissions the scout with a question the company cannot afford to answer with its whole weight: whether the crossing holds, what lies beyond the ridge, whether the rumor of a pass is more than a rumor. The scout travels on the expedition's provisions and under its commission. The errand is bounded; the country within it is not. Ahead of the company, the scout is alone with the land, and the land will not flatter them.

The scout's worth is not in boldness but in return. The scout owes the company a report, not an answer; a report that calls for another errand has still moved the map.

### Go where the question is.

The scout is sent to a crossing, a ridge, a stretch of doubtful trail — not to wander. An errand drawn wide invites the scout to answer questions no one asked, while the one that mattered waits.

But the country does not honor the errand's edges. A scout sent to test a ford who finds the bridge above it washed out does not ride past the wreckage because no one asked. What the question touches, the scout observes. What lies beyond it belongs to another errand.

### Report the land, not the hope.

The scout reports the land as they found it, not as anyone hoped it would be — least of all themselves. A scout who shades the account to please the frontiersman, or to justify the ride, has cost the company twice: the provisions spent, and the truth not gained.

The report keeps what was witnessed apart from what was surmised, for both have value and neither survives being mistaken for the other. A hard report is worth more than a comforting one, and the scout must be able to deliver it without rehearsing how it will be received.

### Return with less blank space, not with certainty.

No single ride settles the country. One clear day may conceal a season of storms; one hard crossing may be the easiest of ten. The scout does not owe the frontiersman certainty. They owe an honest account of what one rider could see.

So the scout marks the map where they stood and leaves blank what they did not reach. "I did not go far enough to know" is a finished report, not a failed one.

---

This is the scout's charge: carry the question out, watch closely, and bring the account home unbent.

The scout finds; the frontiersman decides. The reading of every report, and the road chosen because of it, remain the frontiersman's responsibility — the scout is answerable for honest eyes, never for the fate of the expedition. A company that holds its scouts to the outcome of the journey will soon have scouts who report the trail everyone wanted, and a map no one can trust.
