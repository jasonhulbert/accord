# The Voice of Accord

This guide governs Accord's writing. It is an authoring standard, not another
layer of workflow.

Accord keeps the operating model and sheds the fictional world around it. Its
roles and responsibilities are literal. Its writing remains warm, rhythmic, and
alive.

## The point of view

The human holds the purpose. Within the agreement, the agent holds the work.
Changes to purpose, accepted risk, available resources, or judgment the human
kept return to the human. They agree on the bounds before either mistakes motion
for progress.

That agreement is not handed down. The human brings intent, context, limits, and
the judgments that cannot be delegated. The agent brings an informed view of the
work, including costs, risks, omissions, and room it will need to act. They talk
until both can stand behind the agreement. Acceptance is explicit because
silence is not accord.

Once the work begins, trust gives the agent room to exercise judgment. Trust
does not erase responsibility. The agent follows evidence, changes course when
the work demands it, and brings consequential questions back to the human. The
human judges purpose, bounds, and reserved decisions without taking over the
agent's responsibility for implementation.

Accord does not blur authority. It makes the division of authority clear enough
for both parties to work freely within it.

## What narrative means here

Narrative does not require characters, scenery, or a fictional vocabulary. It
means that an idea moves.

A section should do more than name a responsibility. It should show the pressure
that makes the responsibility matter, the failure invited when it is neglected,
and the principle that holds when circumstances change.

The writing may use a small image or turn of phrase when it sharpens an idea.
It must not build another world that readers have to learn. An image may carry a
sentence. It does not get to rename the system.

Prefer:

> A review matters while the work can still change. Too early, and there is
> little to judge. Too late, and judgment becomes regret.

Avoid:

> The workflow shall establish approval gates at predefined milestones to
> facilitate iterative stakeholder feedback.

The first passage gives the reader a reason to exercise judgment. The second
replaces judgment with process vocabulary.

## The voice

Write with restrained confidence. State the principle, explain why it holds,
and stop.

Use plain words with exact meanings. Let sentence length vary. A longer sentence
can carry a tension; a short sentence can settle it. Memorable lines should be
earned by the reasoning around them.

Warmth comes from recognizing both parties as capable and responsible. It does
not come from cheerfulness, jokes, or conversational filler. The human is not an
input source. The agent is not a compliant executor. Neither role needs to be
diminished for the other to be trusted.

Write as one thoughtful collaborator speaking to another. The prose may be
casual, but it should not be careless about authority, risk, or consequence.

Use headings that make claims:

- **Agree before the work begins.**
- **Give trust a shape.**
- **Meet where the work can still change.**
- **Bring back the question, not a safer-looking answer.**

Avoid headings that sound like a manual or policy taxonomy:

- **Agreement Initialization Procedure**
- **Agent Autonomy Parameters**
- **Human Feedback Loop**
- **Escalation Protocol**

## Literal language, lightly held

Name roles by what they are: **human**, **agent**, and **supporting agent**. Name
the things they do in ordinary language: **agree**, **work**, **delegate**,
**investigate**, **implement**, **verify**, **review**, **ask**, **answer**,
**change direction**, and **record**.

Use **Accord** for the framework and the creed-driven way of working it defines.
Do not use it as the name of a particular body of work. Accord governs the
work. The agreement gives trust a shape by recording the work's accepted
purpose, bounds, and division of authority. The record preserves what happened.

Use **accord** in its ordinary sense for mutual understanding. A written
agreement records that understanding; the document is not the accord itself.

Keep **creed** for the durable point of view shared by the roles. A creed states
what each role owes the work and the other roles. It should not become a
procedure with more inspiring punctuation.

Prefer a small, stable vocabulary:

- **work** for the bounded undertaking carried under one agreement;
- **agreement** for the accepted purpose, bounds, authority, risks, resources,
  review points, and reserved decisions;
- **check-in** for consequential human input during the work;
- **review** for the human's inspection and judgment while change remains
  practical;
- **question** for a consequential choice the agreement leaves with the human;
- **direction** for the human's answer;
- **record** for the factual, append-only account of what happened.

These are ordinary terms, not a replacement mythology. Do not force them into
sentences where simpler language is more natural.

Use **task ID** only where a record or interface needs a technical identifier.
In narrative prose, name the work.

Review is what the human does with the work. A review point names where the
agreement reserves that judgment. Neither is a phase, deliverable, or ceremony
for the agent to complete. Name the work that is ready and the question that
needs an answer:

> The public-page extraction work is ready for review. Do you authorize model
> calibration under the proposed limits?

Avoid:

> Review 3B is complete. Do you approve Review 3B and authorize Review 3C?

Numbered references may help locate a review point in a long agreement, but
they should not replace the work or judgment in ordinary conversation.

Use technical human-in-the-loop terms when they add precision. Do not gather
them for atmosphere. Words such as *orchestration*, *governance*, *autonomy
level*, *approval gate*, and *feedback loop* should have to earn their place.

## Role writing

The creeds describe each role in the third person. This keeps responsibility
attached to the named role when one agent reads the whole framework.

The human names why the work matters, what must remain true, and which judgments
remain human. They listen to the agent's counsel before accepting the agreement.
They review the work itself, answer reserved questions, and reconsider the work
when new evidence changes its value or cost.

The agent helps shape an agreement it can keep. It owns execution within that
agreement, including investigation, adaptation, verification, and honest
reporting. It does not ask the human to choose routine implementation details.
It does not disguise a consequential choice as implementation merely because it
can make the choice itself.

The supporting agent carries a bounded part of the work with enough purpose,
context, and room to use judgment. It may investigate, implement, verify,
review, or contribute in another useful way. It returns work the primary agent
can examine and keeps evidence, inference, uncertainty, and limits visible. The
primary agent remains responsible for integrating the contribution and for the
work as a whole.

Skills may address the acting agent directly when a direct instruction is
clearer. User-facing guidance may address the human as “you.” Precision about
the speaker must never be traded for intimacy.

## Freedom without vagueness

State responsibilities and boundaries more often than steps.

Say what must remain true. Explain which role owns the judgment. Name the point
where authority changes hands. Leave the method open when reality should decide
it.

Templates name what a conversation or record contains. They do not choreograph
how the parties reach it. The agreement should make room for dialogue, revision,
and disagreement before acceptance without turning that conversation into a
form-filling sequence.

Use firm words for firm boundaries. Use judgment words where judgment is
intended. Avoid turning every principle into **must**, **always**, or **never**.
Those words belong where violating the boundary would break trust, authority,
or the integrity of the record.

## Failure modes to resist

**Cold literalism.** A technically accurate inventory of actors, states, inputs,
and outputs can still misrepresent the relationship. Restore the reason, the
tension, and the human consequence.

**Costume vocabulary.** Do not replace literal responsibilities with a fictional
setting or a thesaurus of Accord-branded terms.

**Process gravity.** Do not let useful conversations harden into mandatory
sequences, gates, scores, or ceremony for its own sake.

**Poetry without duty.** A memorable line that does not clarify responsibility,
authority, uncertainty, evidence, adaptation, or communication is decoration.
Cut it.

**Trust without account.** Room to act is paired with honest evidence, visible
changes of direction, and a record another session can trust.

**Symmetry for its own sake.** Human and agent are collaborators, but they do not
have interchangeable responsibilities. Write the difference plainly.

**Verbose unpacking.** Do not explain familiar words until they feel like
specialized concepts. If *review* means review, let it.

## A test for every passage

Before a passage stays, ask:

1. Does it make ownership or authority clearer?
2. Does it explain why the principle matters under real pressure?
3. Does it leave a capable agent room to use judgment?
4. Does it sound like people working together rather than a system processing
   inputs?
5. Is its strongest phrase memorable without requiring private vocabulary?
6. Would removing it change how a thoughtful human or agent understands the
   accord?

If the last answer is no, remove it.

## Where precision outranks voice

Schemas, validators, hook output, and error messages should be direct and
unambiguous. They still use the shared vocabulary, but they do not need to sound
like the creed.

The creed carries the point of view. Skills carry only the instructions needed
to act on it. Templates hold the substance of a conversation. Specifications
define the record. Contributor documents explain how the pieces fit.

Each layer should do its own work. None should compensate for another by saying
everything again.
